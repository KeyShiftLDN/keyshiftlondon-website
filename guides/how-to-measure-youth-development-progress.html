Measuring Impact

How to Measure Youth Development Progress

Key Shift London8 min readPrecision, not provision

Ask a funder what changed for a young person and "they really came out of their shell" will not survive the question that follows. Youth development is real, but if you cannot show it, you cannot defend it, repeat it or fund it.

Measurement is where good youth work most often falls down. The work itself is skilled and genuine; the evidence of it is a folder of anecdotes. This guide sets out how to measure youth development progress in a way that is honest, practical and reportable: what "progress" actually means, why behaviour-based measurement misleads, what to track instead, and how to turn slow identity change into evidence a young person owns and an institution can report.

What does "progress" mean in youth development?

Progress in youth development is not a grade going up. It is a change in how a young person understands and carries themselves: more agency, clearer identity, steadier relationships, a bigger sense of what is possible. These are often called "soft outcomes," which is a misleading name. They are the hardest things to build and the most durable things to lose.

The measurement problem is that these changes are real but not obviously countable. So practitioners reach for the things that are easy to count (attendance, incidents, sessions delivered) and quietly hope they stand in for the development underneath. Sometimes they do. Often they do not. A young person can attend every session and change nothing, or change profoundly and still miss three weeks because of what is happening at home.

Why behaviour-based measurement misleads

The most common approach is to track behaviour: fewer detentions, better attendance, fewer flagged incidents. It is appealing because the data already exists. But behaviour is a lagging, noisy signal. It moves for reasons that have nothing to do with development, and it can stay flat while real change is happening underneath.

Counting behaviour tells you what a young person did last term. It rarely tells you who they are becoming, and that is the thing you are actually trying to change.

This is the same principle that sits underneath our whole method: behaviour is the weather, identity is the climate. If your measurement only watches the weather, you will be fooled by a good week and panicked by a bad one. To measure development, you have to measure at the level development actually happens: identity.

What to measure: identity, not just outcomes

At Key Shift London we start measurement where we start the work, with identity. Our SHAPE model maps the five environments that shape a young person's sense of self: School, Home, Aspirations, Perspectives and Experiences. Measured over time, SHAPE gives you a moving picture of the young person's world, not a snapshot of their conduct.

In practice, that means capturing a baseline read across the five SHAPE dimensions, then revisiting it as the relationship develops. You are not scoring the young person out of ten. You are tracking direction: where pressure is easing, where a flattened aspiration is lifting, where a guarded perspective is opening. A shift in Aspirations (a young person who starts to believe a future is actually available to them) is a more meaningful marker of progress than a term of clean attendance, even though attendance is easier to put in a spreadsheet.

A practical framework for tracking progress

Measurement should be built into the work, not bolted on at the end. In Precision Training we teach a simple, repeatable rhythm that turns everyday practice into evidence without turning practitioners into administrators.

  • Baseline. At the start, map where the young person actually is across the SHAPE environments. This is your reference point. Without it, every later "improvement" is a guess.
  • Define the shift. Name, with the young person where possible, what growth would look like for them, not a generic target. Progress you have not defined is progress you cannot claim.
  • Capture signal as you go. Log what you observe session by session (a changed belief, a risk taken, a relationship repaired) against the dimension it touches. Small, dated, specific. This is the raw material of evidence.
  • Review the direction. At intervals, step back and read the trend across the whole picture. You are looking for movement and its causes, not a single number.
  • Report the story with the evidence. Pair the human change with the dated signal behind it. That combination, narrative plus trail, is what survives a funder's follow-up question.

Done this way, measurement stops being a separate task you dread and becomes a by-product of good practice. You are not measuring instead of working; you are capturing the work you are already doing.

Hold progress over perfection

Identity does not move in a single session, and measurement that expects it to will read every slow month as failure. The point of tracking progress is not to prove rapid transformation; it is to make slow, real change visible so it can be trusted and continued. Expect plateaus. Expect regressions when something shifts at home. A good measurement system shows you the line of travel across months, so a hard week does not get mistaken for a lost cause.

Common measurement mistakes

  • Measuring only what is easy to count. Attendance and incidents are cheap to collect and weak as evidence of development. Use them as context, not as proof.
  • No baseline. Without a starting read, you can describe a young person's state but never their change.
  • Measuring at the end. Evidence reconstructed from memory months later is thin and unconvincing. Capture as you go.
  • Confusing activity with impact. Sessions delivered is a measure of effort, not of outcome. A full programme can change very little.
  • Numbers with no story. A dashboard with no human narrative behind it is as weak as a story with no evidence. Progress is proven by both together.

Frequently asked questions

How do you measure "soft" outcomes in youth work?

By measuring at the level of identity rather than behaviour. Map a baseline across the dimensions that shape a young person (Key Shift London uses the SHAPE model: School, Home, Aspirations, Perspectives, Experiences), capture dated, specific signal as the work progresses, and review the direction of travel over time. The result is evidence that pairs a human story with a trackable trail.

What is the difference between outputs and outcomes?

Outputs are what you deliver: sessions run, young people reached, hours of mentoring. Outcomes are what changes as a result: in confidence, identity, relationships or aspiration. Funders increasingly ask for outcomes. Counting outputs alone measures your effort, not your impact.

How often should you measure progress?

Capture signal continuously, as part of ordinary practice, and review the overall picture at sensible intervals (for example each half-term or at the close of a coaching cycle). Continuous capture avoids reconstructing evidence from memory; periodic review keeps you focused on the trend rather than the last session.

Can you prove youth development progress to a funder or commissioner?

Yes, if the evidence is built in from the start. A defensible report pairs the narrative of what changed with the dated, specific signal behind it, mapped to a baseline. This is what PRE:CORD is designed to produce: reportable evidence a young person owns and an institution can stand behind.
